I. Introduction

This Coding Handbook documents the pilot implementation of the Contestability Measurement Framework and the coding records underlying the measures. Section II sets out the De Jure scoring criteria, data methodology, and legal-form classification. Section III specifies the De Facto scoring rules, indicator structure, market-specific adaptation, gatekeeper selection, and evidence standards. Section IV reports the jurisdiction-level De Jure comparison and presents the factor-level scores, rationales, market coverage, and legal bases for each jurisdiction. Section V applies the framework to the pilot gatekeeper–market observations. For each application, it defines the market scope and applicable mechanisms, reports the aligned market-level De Jure and De Facto assessments, and presents Effective Contestability as a derived summary of the two layers.

A dedicated project database tracks the full lifecycle of the underlying data. For each item, it records the source (the issuing authority and official publication), the raw collected document, the processed data used for analysis, and a complete version history recording when entries were collected, reviewed, and revised across snapshots. As a result, every entry reported in this Coding Handbook is fully traceable, from the final score back through the processed text to the original official document. The database contents specific to each measure are described in the corresponding section.

II. De Jure Contestability

This section sets out how De Jure Contestability is coded. It defines the scoring criteria applied to every mechanism–jurisdiction cell, describes the data methodology that produces the underlying record, and sets out the legal-form classification used to keep instruments comparable across very different legal systems. The coded results are reported in Section IV.

A. Scoring criteria

Each mechanism–jurisdiction cell rests on three factors, each scored on the same 0 / 0.5 / 1 grid: Existence (whether the relevant obligation is present in the enacted framework), Scope (the range of firms, services, and activities covered), and Enforceability (whether the provision is backed by credible oversight and sanctions).

FactorScore 0Score 0.5Score 1
Existence — is an operative obligation on point present in the enacted framework?Absent: no binding obligation on point; general competition/consumer law only; drafts; or a designation framework under which no obligation has yet been imposed.Present but incomplete or formally weak: partial or adjacent coverage; a statutory catalogue still requiring case-by-case activation; administrative guidance of weak formal status; or case-specific precedents/commitments only.A binding, enacted obligation on point whose content is fixed ex ante by the instrument itself (statute, regulation, court order, or imposed conduct requirement).
Scope — what range of firms, services and activities does the provision reach?No coverage: the instrument reaches no relevant firm, service or activity. Scope does not fall to 0 automatically when Existence = 0. Under legal form F5 an enacted designation framework fixes the addressees without fixing an obligation on point, so Scope may be recorded as 0.5 and Enforceability as 0.5; the final score is 0 through the minimum rule.A subset of the provision’s domain: firm-specific instruments (remedies, designations, conduct requirements), or sectoral/market slices covering only part of the mechanism’s economic domain.A rule of general application covering the mechanism’s full economic domain within the jurisdiction.
Enforceability — is the provision supported by credible oversight and sanctions?No enforcement mechanism.Enforcement requires fresh case-by-case proceedings; guidance without penalties or private enforcement; or machinery untested / not yet effective.A dedicated regulator (or supervising court) with penalty powers; the obligation is self-executing or directly enforceable, including private enforcement where available.

Final provision score = MIN(Existence, Scope, Enforceability). The minimum is used because the factors do not compensate for one another: an obligation that does not exist is not helped by strong enforcement machinery; a well-drafted obligation binding a single firm is still not a market-wide rule; a broad rule without credible enforcement offers little protection in practice. Scope is judged against the mechanism’s own economic domain rather than the whole economy — a marketplace-only statute covers the full domain of a marketplace-native mechanism (Scope = 1 there) but only part of the domain of a mechanism that operates economy-wide (Scope = 0.5).

B. Data methodology

The sixteen mechanisms are grouped under the four dimensions shown in Section IV.A. A mechanism is a stable contestability channel rather than a provision of any single statute; where a channel can be secured through more than one route (for example, alternative distribution through either sideloading or third-party app stores, or technical interoperability through either API access or hardware access), the routes are treated as one mechanism and scored on the strongest available channel.

The data were collected in four steps, so that every score can be traced to a named legal instrument and every instrument to its official text. First, for each jurisdiction the digital-competition instruments in force or pending as of June 2026 (statutes, regulations, court remedies, conduct requirements, administrative rules) were registered with citations and links to official sources, together with a mapping from each mechanism to the articles that govern it. Second, for each mechanism the operative text of every instrument bearing on it was copied verbatim from the primary source, with English translations where needed; scoring works from this primary text, not from secondary summaries. Third, each mechanism–jurisdiction pair was scored on the three factors set out in Section II.A. Fourth, the mechanism scores were averaged into dimension scores and then into the De Jure Contestability Index reported in Section IV.A. The records produced at each step are kept in the project database.

For the De Jure layer, the project database described in the introduction will include: (i) the source register — every statute, regulation, court remedy, conduct requirement, and administrative rule bearing on the sixteen mechanisms, with citation, status, official-source link, and the pending/pipeline instruments tracked alongside those in force; (ii) the original documents — the official texts as published (official gazettes, EUR-Lex, e-Gov, court dockets), stored in the original language; (iii) the processed versions — the cleaned machine-readable texts and the verbatim provision extracts, with English translations, that scoring works from; (iv) the assessments — all 144 mechanism × jurisdiction records with the three factor scores, the final score, the rationale, the targeted market, and the legal basis, plus the market-coverage matrix; and (v) the history — the review status of each record and its changes across snapshots as instruments are enacted, amended, activated by orders, or repealed (for example, a pipeline bill moving to in force).

C. Legal-form classification

To keep the factor signatures comparable across very different legal systems, instruments are classified into one of eight mutually exclusive legal forms; each form has a typical factor signature from which its score ceiling follows under the MIN rule. This is why, outside the EU and Germany, most non-zero cells sit at 0.5: they rest on firm-specific orders, sectoral statutes, guidance, or case practice rather than on generally applicable ex ante statutes.

III. De Facto Contestability

This section sets out how De Facto Contestability is coded. It states the scoring criteria, specifies the indicator set, sets out how the set is adapted from one market to the next, states how the gatekeeper and market are selected, and describes the evidence standards. These rules are shared across markets. The market applications are reported in Section V.

A. Scoring criteria

Each dimension is decomposed into De Facto indicators drawn from the Methodology Manual and scored on the 0 / 0.5 / 1 grid used throughout the Index. Each indicator is scored twice, once for the pre-DMA baseline and once for the post-DMA state, so that the change isolates the effect of the regulation from the pre-existing level.

Scores are assigned by the following rule.

ScoreCondition
0The mechanism is absent, prohibited, or obstructed to a degree that leaves no working path.
0.5A working path exists, subject to friction the gatekeeper imposes or maintains. Fees, warning screens, multi-step procedures, single-presentation prompts, withheld data and unverified self-reporting all fall here.
1The mechanism operates with no gatekeeper-imposed friction.

Before scoring, each indicator is assigned an applicability status. Applicable means the mechanism operates in the market. Not Applicable means it does not, and the indicator is excluded from aggregation rather than scored zero, since non-applicability carries no information about contestability. Applicable but Missing means the mechanism operates but the evidence required to score it has not been obtained; the indicator is excluded from the denominator and the reason is recorded. Missing status is used where a score would otherwise depend on inference, on general knowledge of a platform, or on the absence of an adverse regulatory finding.

Dimension scores are the unweighted arithmetic mean of the applicable indicators scored. The De Facto Contestability Index is the equal-weighted average of the four dimension scores. The De Jure layer takes the minimum of Existence, Scope and Enforceability because a missing obligation is not cured by enforcement machinery. The De Facto layer averages, because partial progress on several channels adds to observed contestability.

Formal availability does not establish adoption. Where an indicator concerns use or market behaviour, platform documentation alone supports no score above 0.5.

Market-share persistence is excluded as a scored dimension. Following Furman et al. (2019), market share is an outcome of contestability rather than a dimension of it, and including it would introduce circularity into the Index.

The set is not extended with market-specific composites: aggregate measures such as cost of entry, or structural infrastructure costs not imposed by the gatekeeper, are recorded in the evidence column rather than scored.

C. Market-specific variation in the indicator set

The four dimensions and sixteen mechanisms are stable and do not vary. Three things vary across markets.

Applicability. Some mechanisms do not operate in a given market. Cross-platform messaging connectivity applies to number-independent interpersonal communications and to no other market coded here. Parity applies to app stores and intermediation, and reaches search through Shopping, which sits inside the market boundary. Anti-steering reaches any market whose business users are acquired through a Core Platform Service, and so applies in search, though the conduct it observes there differs. Applicability is fixed before scoring and held constant across jurisdictions and periods within a market.

Observation. The same mechanism is observed through different evidence. Default switching is observed in search through choice-screen deployment and the number of steps to change an engine, and in app distribution through the range of reassignable default categories and whether the store is among them.

Competitive weight. The same indicator carries different economic weight across markets. Multi-homing signals little in search, where a user running two engines still sends most queries to one, and signals more in social networking, where accounts on several services coexist and attention divides. Data portability matters more in social networking, where the social graph is the asset that holds users, than in search. Interoperability binds in messaging and operating systems and matters less in marketplaces. Under equal weighting these differences appear in the evidence and in score dispersion, not in the weights. A market-specific weighting scheme requires justification and documentation.

De Facto scores are comparable across jurisdictions and periods within a market, and across gatekeepers within a market where the same indicator set has been applied. They are not comparable across markets. An index of 0.50 in app distribution and 0.50 in search describe different conditions reached through different mechanisms. Cross-market statements should be made at the level of the mechanism.

D. Gatekeeper and market selection

De Facto Contestability is measured for a gatekeeper–market pair. The Methodology Manual requires the gatekeeper to be identified as part of the observation where competitive conditions are platform-specific. An indicator set applies only where the named firm operates the bottleneck the mechanism describes.

A gatekeeper enters a market observation only where it controls a Core Platform Service in that market. Scoring Amazon in online search would be an error of classification: Amazon operates no general search engine, the search indicators would have no object, and the resulting score would measure non-participation rather than low contestability. Alphabet holds the search bottleneck in the European Union. Amazon is the subject in online marketplaces, Meta in social networking and number-independent messaging, and Apple and Alphabet in app distribution and mobile operating systems.

Where a market has more than one gatekeeper, each is coded separately on the same indicator set and reported alongside the other. Averaging them would remove the variation the measure is intended to record. A market-level summary across gatekeepers requires an explicit weighting rule for the relative economic importance of each ecosystem.

Three consequences follow. Applicability is determined against the named gatekeeper’s service. Evidence must concern that gatekeeper; a decision addressed to Apple establishes nothing about Alphabet. And a pre-period score of 0 is distinct from non-participation: Apple scores 0 on alternative distribution in December 2023 because it prohibited the channel, which is a finding about contestability, while a firm operating no service in the market is not scored.

E. Evidence standards

Each pair is scored from Commission non-compliance decisions, preliminary findings and specification decisions; gatekeeper compliance reports, developer documentation and published fee schedules; national competition and data-authority actions; judgments of the Court of Justice; and market data from independent providers. Every cell is traceable from its score to the originating document.

A regulatory penalty is not by itself evidence of a market condition. A gatekeeper may be fined because its conduct did not change, in which case the coding is unchanged. A score moves only on documented change in the observable condition. Where a penalty decision also records implemented changes, those records are the evidence, and the decision is cited for the finding that friction persists.

Announced measures that were not operative during the observation window are recorded in the evidence column and do not affect the score.

Three pairs are coded in this version: Alphabet in online search, Apple in iOS app distribution, and Alphabet in Android app distribution. The two app-distribution observations use one indicator set and are reported together so the ecosystems can be compared.

V. Market–Jurisdiction Applications

This section applies the framework market by market. It reports the De Facto Contestability layer of the Index: the observed contestability of specific gatekeeper–market pairs, measured by the extent to which enacted obligations have translated into changes in market conditions in practice. Where the De Jure layer scores the law as written across nine jurisdictions, the De Facto layer scores conduct and outcomes for individual Core Platform Services (CPS), comparing a pre-DMA baseline (December 2023) with the latest observed state (July 2026); the scoring criteria and evidence base it applies are set out in Section III. For each market, the section first characterizes the market itself, then presents the full De Facto scoring table for the gatekeeper–market pair — the sub-indicator scores, the pre/post comparison, and the supporting evidence for every cell — restates the market-level De Jure Contestability score — both as it stands in June 2026 and against a December 2023 pre-DMA baseline, so that the legal layer can be read as a movement rather than a level — and finally combines the layers into the market’s Effective Contestability. Two EU markets are coded in full, across three gatekeeper–market pairs: online search (Google Search), and app distribution (Apple App Store and Google Play). The De Jure baseline is coded for Google Search and the Apple App Store, the two pairs for which a market-level De Jure catalogue is reported.

Market: online search. Assessed service: Google Search (Alphabet Inc.), designated as a Core Platform Service under the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA) on 6 September 2023.

1. Market scope and applicable mechanisms

The standardized market is online search, assessed through Google Search, a designated Core Platform Service under the DMA. Applicable mechanisms are identified separately for the two layers. At the De Jure layer, all sixteen mechanisms are scored: in the European Union the DMA and the Platform-to-Business Regulation impose a complete set of obligations on the designated service. At the De Facto layer, the set narrows. De Jure scoring credits an obligation wherever it binds Alphabet as a designated gatekeeper, including where its operative locus is app distribution, the operating system or messaging; De Facto scoring observes only conduct in the search service itself, so a mechanism operating in an adjacent market has no object here. Technical interoperability (D4.1) and cross-platform messaging connectivity (D4.2) are therefore Not Applicable, and the remaining fourteen indicators apply. Applicability is fixed before scoring and held constant across both periods (Section III.C). Which of the fourteen could then be scored on the available evidence is a separate question, reported with the scoring table in Section V.A.3.
